Light, eyes and cameras: where an image comes from

Before an image becomes an array of numbers it is light: a spread of wavelengths arriving from every direction. This page follows that light into the eye and into a camera. Both throw most of it away, and in surprisingly similar ways. The leftovers, three numbers per point, are what we call colour and what an image file stores.

01

The plenoptic function: all the light there is

Stand still and look around. What you could possibly see is the amount of light arriving from every direction. Write a direction with two angles, \(\theta\) (left–right) and \(\phi\) (up–down). Light also comes in different wavelengths \(\lambda\), it changes with time \(t\), and it is different if you move your eye to another point \((V_x, V_y, V_z)\). Adelson and Bergen (1991) called the function of all seven variables the plenoptic function (from Latin plenus, full, and optic):

\[ P(\theta,\, \phi,\, \lambda,\, t,\, V_x,\, V_y,\, V_z) = \text{intensity of light of wavelength } \lambda \text{ arriving at } (V_x,V_y,V_z) \text{ from direction } (\theta,\phi) \text{ at time } t . \]

\(V\) is a place, not a motion. \((V_x, V_y, V_z)\) is the viewpoint: where the eye, or the camera's lens centre, sits in space. It is not a velocity. Turning your eyes or head on the spot keeps \(V\) (almost) fixed and only changes which directions \((\theta,\phi)\) you look at, so nothing new appears. Walking somewhere else changes \(V\): near objects shift against far ones (parallax) and hidden things come into view. Your two eyes are two viewpoints about 6.5 cm apart, and that small difference in \(V\) is what stereo depth uses. Movement through the scene is a viewpoint that changes with time, \(V(t)\).

It holds every photograph, every movie and every view anyone could ever see. No device records all of it. Every imaging device records a slice: it fixes some variables, keeps a few samples of others, and adds up the rest.

Demo 1 · Which slice does a device record?Tick the variables the device keeps. The count assumes a 4000 × 3000 grid of directions.

An ordinary photo is the smallest slice: one viewpoint, one instant, and the whole spectrum squeezed into three numbers. Sections 3 to 6 show why three numbers are enough for us, and how both the eye and the camera make them.

02

The eye: a camera made of tissue

The eye and a camera solve the same problem with the same parts: bend the light to form an image, control how much gets in, and turn the image into signals.

JobEyeCamera
Bend light into an imagecornea (most of the bending) and lenslens
Focusthe lens changes shapethe lens moves
Control the lightiris: the pupil is about 2–8 mm wideaperture (f-number) and exposure time
Turn light into signalsabout 92 million rods and 4.6 million cones (Curcio et al., 1990)millions of photosites
Send the signal onabout 1 million ganglion-cell fibres in the optic nervea data bus

Two numbers in that table deserve a second look. About 97 million receptors feed about 1 million nerve fibres, so the retina compresses the image roughly a hundredfold before it leaves the eye. And the two types of receptor do different jobs. Cones work in bright light and see colour. Rods are far more sensitive, work at night, and see only shades of grey. That is why colours fade on a moonlit walk.

The receptors are not spread evenly. Cones are packed into the fovea, the small pit that gives sharp central vision; rods take over in the periphery. Hover over the plot.

One stretch of the plot is empty. The optic nerve leaves the eye about 15° from the fovea, towards the nose, and there are no receptors there at all. Each eye has a blind spot about 5° wide, larger than ten full moons side by side. You can find yours.

Demo 3 · Find your blind spotClose your left eye. Look at the cross with your right eye. Move slowly towards or away from the screen.

At a distance of about 3½ times the gap between the cross and the dot, the dot disappears. With the line or the stripes, notice what you see in its place: the brain fills the hole with the surrounding pattern. You never notice your blind spot in daily life because the other eye covers it, and because of this filling-in.

Fields of view: one eye covers about 160° horizontally and 135° vertically, and the two eyes together about 200° by 135°. Only the 2° of the fovea is sharp, which is why the eyes keep moving; the gaze module follows those movements.

03

Light is a spectrum

Any patch of light is completely described, physically, by its spectrum: how much power (or how many photons per second) it carries at each wavelength between about 400 and 700 nm. A laser puts everything at one wavelength. A hot filament spreads its light smoothly, more towards red the cooler it is. A white LED is a blue chip plus a yellow phosphor.

Most things we see do not make light; they reflect it. A surface reflects a fraction \(R(\lambda)\) of each wavelength. The light reaching your eye from it is the product of the illuminant \(E(\lambda)\) and the reflectance:

\[ C(\lambda) = E(\lambda)\,R(\lambda). \]

So the same banana sends different light to your eye at noon and under a street lamp. Try it.

Three things to try. Under the sodium lamp, every surface becomes the same orange; only its brightness changes. A one-wavelength light cannot show colour, and adapting to it does not help: you get shades of grey. Under the tungsten bulb everything turns orange, white paper included. Now tick adapt to the light: the colours come back. Your brain does this adaptation all the time, and a camera does it with its white-balance setting.

04

Three cones, three numbers

A spectrum has a value at every wavelength, but the eye keeps only three numbers per point. There are three types of cone: L, M and S (long, medium and short wavelength, loosely "red", "green" and "blue"). Each type adds up the incoming light weighted by its own sensitivity curve:

\[ L = \int C(\lambda)\,\bar l(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \qquad M = \int C(\lambda)\,\bar m(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \qquad S = \int C(\lambda)\,\bar s(\lambda)\,d\lambda . \]

That is a projection: an infinite-dimensional spectrum squeezed onto three numbers. Two different spectra that give the same \((L, M, S)\) look exactly the same. They are called metamers, and they are why a screen with only red, green and blue dots can show you a yellow flower.

The overall sensitivity of the eye in daylight, the sum that sets how bright things look, peaks at about 555 nm, a yellowish green. Green light looks brighter than blue or red light of the same power. Remember this for the Bayer filter in Section 6.

The screen's primaries are modelled as smooth humps peaking near 615, 535 and 455 nm, roughly like an LED or OLED display. Cone curves are estimated from the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (analytic fit by Wyman, Sloan and Shirley, 2013) with the Hunt–Pointer–Estévez matrix.

Look at the left plot: the two spectra have almost nothing in common, yet the cone bars agree and the swatches match. That is a metamer, and it is all a screen ever does. It never copies the spectrum of the flower, only its three cone numbers. The three amounts it uses are the R, G, B values stored in the image file (Section 6).

Now move the target to about 490–510 nm (cyan-green). The screen's red has to go negative, which no screen can do. With red switched off, the mix is paler and the bars no longer agree. Every display covers only a triangle of the colours we can see, its gamut; a pure spectral colour always lies just outside it.

Is more cones better? The mantis shrimp is often called the champion of colour vision: it has 12 types of colour receptor (16 photoreceptor types in all, some for ultraviolet and polarised light), against our 3. Yet when Thoen and colleagues (2014) tested them, the shrimp could not tell apart colours 12–25 nm apart, which humans separate easily. The shrimp seems to recognise colours directly, one receptor type each, instead of comparing receptors as we do. More sensors is not the same as finer discrimination; how the numbers are compared matters too.

05

A sensor counts photons

A camera sensor is a grid of photosites. During the exposure time \(T\) each one counts the photons that land on it. Photons arrive at random, so even a perfectly steady light gives a count that varies from shot to shot. The count follows a Poisson distribution, whose spread is the square root of its mean:

\[ n \sim \text{Poisson}(\bar n), \qquad \bar n \propto \text{brightness} \times T, \qquad \text{SNR} = \frac{\bar n}{\sqrt{\bar n}} = \sqrt{\bar n}. \]

The count is then turned into a number with \(N\) bits, one of \(2^N\) levels. These are the same two steps as for sound, sampling (here: in space, on the photosite grid) and quantization (in level). The rest of this section unpacks the SNR formula, and why few photons show up as grain rather than just darkness.

Where \(\sqrt{\bar n}\) comes from

Point one photosite at a steady light. Chop the exposure into a huge number \(K\) of tiny time slots, so short that each holds at most one photon. In each slot a photon arrives with a small probability \(p\), independently of every other slot. The count \(n\) is the number of slots that got a photon: a sum of \(K\) independent yes/no events.

One slot contributes 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 otherwise, so its mean is \(p\) and its variance is \(p(1-p)\). For a sum of independent events, the means add and the variances add:

\[ \bar n = Kp, \qquad \sigma^2 = Kp(1-p) \approx Kp = \bar n \quad (\text{because } p \text{ is tiny}) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\bar n}. \]

The variance equals the mean; that is the defining property of the Poisson distribution. The signal is the average count \(\bar n\), the noise is the typical swing \(\sigma\), so

\[ \text{SNR} = \frac{\bar n}{\sigma} = \frac{\bar n}{\sqrt{\bar n}} = \sqrt{\bar n}, \qquad \text{relative error} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar n}}. \]

More light brings more noise in absolute terms (±100 against ±3), but far less noise compared with the signal. That ratio is what the eye judges.

Why few photons look grainy, not just dark

Each photosite draws its own random count, independently of its neighbours. Picture a plain grey wall at night, with every pixel expecting 9 photons. If every pixel got exactly 9, you would see a smooth, dark wall. Instead the pixels get 7, 12, 9, 5, 11, 14, 6 … The flat wall turns into speckle, and that pixel-to-pixel speckle is the grain.

Then the camera makes it visible. A night photo should not come out black, so the camera multiplies every count by a large gain (that is what raising the ISO does). The gain scales the signal and its swings by the same factor, so the 33% relative error stays. The picture is now bright enough for you to see it. Gain cannot remove noise; only more photons can: a longer exposure, a wider aperture or a bigger photosite.

Demo 6 · One grey wall, many pixelsEvery pixel looks at the same evenly lit wall and counts its photons

Try 9 photons, then 10,000. Then switch the gain off: at night the wall is almost black, and the grain hides in the dark until the gain brings it up.

Two more effects add to this in a real camera. With a Bayer filter (Section 6) the red, green and blue photosites fluctuate independently, so night grain is coloured. And the sensor's own electronics add a small fixed amount of read noise to every pixel, which matters most when the photon count is small. Now the same thing on a photo:

Halving the exposure halves the photon count but cuts the signal-to-noise ratio only by \(\sqrt 2\). Now set plenty of photons and drop the bits to 2 or 3: even a noise-free picture breaks into flat bands of colour. Noise and quantization are two separate losses. A later module picks up the array these numbers fill.

06

The Bayer filter: colour from one sensor

A photosite counts photons; it cannot tell their wavelength. To see colour, most cameras put a tiny colour filter over each photosite, in the pattern Bryce Bayer patented at Kodak in 1976:

RGGB

Each 2 × 2 block has one red, one blue and two green filters, so half of all photosites are green. That matches the eye: we are most sensitive near green, and green carries most of the fine detail we notice. The sensor records only one colour at each pixel, the raw mosaic. The camera then estimates the two missing colours at every pixel from the neighbours. This step is called demosaicing. The simplest version averages the nearest pixels of the right colour:

\[ \hat G = \tfrac14\big(G_{\uparrow}+G_{\downarrow}+G_{\leftarrow}+G_{\rightarrow}\big) \text{ at a red or blue pixel}, \quad \hat R, \hat B: \text{ average of the 2 or 4 nearest red (blue) pixels.} \]

Two-thirds of the numbers in every colour photo are estimates. Usually you cannot tell, but fine stripes give the game away.

Demo 7 · Mosaic and demosaicHover over any image to magnify the same spot in all three

On the photos, the demosaiced image is hard to tell from the scene unless you zoom in on sharp edges. On the fine stripes, colours appear that are not there at all (false colour) and edges get a zip-like pattern. Real cameras use smarter demosaicing that follows edges, and many add a slightly blurring filter in front of the sensor to keep stripes this fine from reaching it. The only R / G / B buttons show the three channels that every colour image file stores.

07

Do we see what is out there?

A camera reports the number of photons. The visual system reports what it thinks the surface is like. Those are different questions, and the difference shows up as illusions. In the picture below, tile A is a dark tile in the light, and tile B is a light tile in a shadow.

Demo 8 · Same pixels, different greysHover to read the pixel value under the pointer
Hover over the checkerboard.

A and B have exactly the same pixel value. Your visual system discounts the shadow, because a light tile in shadow is what such a pattern almost always means in the real world. This is the same adaptation as the white balance in Demo 4, and the reason the brain is good at recognising objects under any light. Other famous cases: the Thatcher illusion (Thompson, 1980), where an upside-down face with its eyes and mouth flipped looks normal until you turn it the right way up, and the "dress" photo of 2015 that people saw as blue–black or white–gold depending on the light they assumed.

Why this matters for image processing. The numbers in an image are photon counts, not surface colours. Two pixels with the same value can belong to different materials, and one material can give very different values. Much of computer vision is the work of undoing the light to recover the surface.
